Welcome!

The Winter Data Analysis Challenge is a chance for undergraduate students to showcase skills and creativity in analysing a complex and challenging dataset. We recognise that great data science brings together development and implementation of algorithms, exploration of the data (including wrangling), and understanding of the wider context and leveraging external information.

The annual Winter Data Analysis Challenge will be open from 20 - 22 July 2026 for undergraduate students.

Students can participate individually or in a team of up to three students.

This is a unique opportunity to examine interesting data outside of formal units of study, with judge- and peer-mentoring during the Challenge. Prizes will be awarded to top submissions at the Challenge celebration for all participants early in Semester 2. Prizes are valued at: 1st prize $1,000, 2nd $500, and 3rd $200.

Thank you to Westpac Institutional Bank and Canva for supporting innovation and learning as a Silver Sponsors of the 2026 Winter Data Analysis Challenge.

Judging criteria
The goal is to value data analysis beyond a single metric (e.g. accuracy). Submissions will be judged according to:

analytical innovation

clarity of communication

critical assessment and evaluation

demonstration of understanding the wider context

The dataset
In this year’s Winter Data Analysis Challenge, you are expected to answer the following question:

Is Australia doing well?

Sound vague?

That’s kind of the point! There are many ways to interpret this question, all of which can be answered using data. You are expected to refine this general question down to something specific and answerable.

There are many domains in which Australia’s performance can be quantified. Some examples are:

Economic: Has Australia’s economic position been improving?

Health: Is Australia a healthy country?

Social: Is Australia tackling inequality?

Well-Being: Do Australians feel happy?

The challenge
We have provided some data on statistics for different countries, as compiled by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) – which is the first thing that came to mind for the organising panel. You are free to use these data, or you can ignore them completely and find your own relevant data in another domain (e.g., economics).

The data come from the OECD’s ‘How’s Life?’ database, and contain national average statistics on material wealth (e.g. income), quality of life (e.g. health) and community (e.g. work-life balance). The data are present for Australia as well as many other nations, and cover a time-series from 2010 to 2024. Hence, we might be able to compare Australia’s improvements in statistics over time and/or its performance internationally or among select peers.

Details and definitions of the contents of the dataset can be found in the attached data guide.
