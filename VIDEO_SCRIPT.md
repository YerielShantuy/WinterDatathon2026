## 0:00–0:20 — Hook + the question

**NARRATION**
Australia consistently ranks among the world's most prosperous nations. High incomes, strong jobs, long lives. So when the datathon asks — is Australia doing well? — the easy answer is yes. We looked closer, and found a country living two very different stories at once. We call it the lived-reality paradox — one country, two divergent realities.

## 0:20–0:45 — The data, and the pooling catch

**NARRATION**
Our source is the OECD's How's Life? database — 47 countries, 2010 to 2024, nine core indicators. But first, a warning we had to fix. The OECD's subjective series are three-year pooled Gallup samples. Sixteen apparent annual points are really six; roughly two-thirds of the year-to-year movement was just forward-filled repetition. We corrected for it before drawing a single conclusion — credibility first.

## 0:45–1:10 — The split (72 vs 38)

**NARRATION**
Here's the split. Ranked against OECD peers in 2023, Australia's objective well-being sits at the seventy-second percentile — its lived well-being at just the thirty-eighth. A thirty-four-point gap. We score near the top on disposable income and life expectancy, around the seventy-ninth percentile. But on long working hours we fall to the seventeenth. Prosperity is real; it just isn't being felt evenly.

## 1:10–1:40 — The divergence, and the regression

**NARRATION**
And the gap is widening. Indexed to 2010, by 2024 deaths of despair are up forty-nine percent and negative affect up twenty-one — while disposable income rose just thirteen and employment six. The macro and the lived are pulling apart. The trajectory is starker still. Since 2010, Australia's percentile rank for social support fell from eighty-fifth to forty-seventh, deaths of despair from seventieth to thirty-ninth, negative affect from forty-ninth to twenty-sixth. On what people actually live, we regressed.

## 1:40–2:10 — The fragility (Monte Carlo)

**NARRATION**
But could we be cherry-picking the weights? We tested it. Running a thousand random weightings across the nine indicators, Australia's composite rank swings anywhere from eighth to forty-first. Equal weights land it at twenty-first. The honest takeaway: any single national ranking is a weighting choice dressed up as fact. The number you quote depends entirely on what you decided to value — which is exactly why headline rankings deserve far more analysis than they usually get.

**[ON SCREEN: Chart 4 — Monte Carlo rank distribution, 8th→41st spread, equal-weight marker at 21st]**

## 2:10–2:35 — Wider context

**NARRATION**
This isn't only an OECD story. Australian household debt sits near two hundred and ten percent of disposable income — among the highest in the OECD, above New Zealand, the UK, and the US. HILDA data echoes it: housing stress, loneliness at twenty-seven percent, financial stress hitting one in eight households. And Treasury's own Measuring What Matters framework now tracks these very domains.

**[ON SCREEN: Chart 6 — peer household-debt comparison; HILDA + MWM context strip]**

## 2:35–3:00 — Conclusion

**NARRATION**
So — is Australia doing well? On paper, yes; in lived experience, less so, and slipping. Prosperity is up, lived experience is down, and the ranking itself is too fragile to trust. Our answer is a challenge: stop grading a nation by its averages, and start measuring what people actually live. Mind the gap — because that gap, not the average, is where the real story lives.
